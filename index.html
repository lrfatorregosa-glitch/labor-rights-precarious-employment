The five-month contract

This is not a marginal practice. According to official establishment surveys, agency-hired and non-regular workers made up between 20% and 30% of the formal private-sector workforce from 2018 through 2025. That is roughly 4.5 to 6 million people (PSA, 2024). They are floor clerks, assembly line operators, warehouse handlers and farmworkers (PSA, 2024).

The 1987 Constitution promises secure, dignified employment. Labor subcontracting is the way that promise is routinely avoided. Global buyers press domestic suppliers to cut costs, the Labor Code's rules on contracting leave room to hide labor-only arrangements inside service agreements, and the state has too few inspectors to catch them (Dayego et al., 2025; ILO, 2023).

This paper asks a synthesis question: when the evidence from law, labor groups, employers, the media and other countries is read together, what actually closes the gap between the rule and the practice? Our answer is that no single tool does. Legal bans stall in court, and tax incentives are voluntary. We propose pairing a strict ban on outsourcing core functions with Smart Escrow Factory-Gate Financing, which ties the money in a supply chain to proof of direct, regular employment.

The revolving door

A supply-chain cost problem that the law's gray areas turn into a job-security problem.

Labor subcontracting in the Philippines is a fundamental challenge to the constitutional mandate of secure, dignified employment. Global supply chain pressure leads domestic suppliers to adopt low-cost, flexible employment to protect their margins (Dayego et al., 2025; ILO, 2023).

Article 106 of the Labor Code makes this easy. Trilateral arrangements let principal enterprises pass legal responsibility to third-party manpower agencies, and labor-only subcontracting hides behind what looks like a legitimate service agreement (Dayego et al., 2025). DOLE runs routine compliance audits under Article 128. Even so, corporate lobbying and weak enforcement keep non-compliant subcontracting alive in retail, electronics, logistics and export agriculture (Dayego et al., 2025; PSA, 2024).

Floor clerks, assembly line operators, warehouse handlers and farmworkers carry most of the short-term cycles.

The "5-5-5" arrangement ends employment contracts at the five-month mark to avoid mandatory regularization at six months under Article 281. It is concentrated among lower-tier service and production roles. Floor clerks, assembly line operators, warehouse handlers and agricultural farmworkers carry most of the short-term cycles (Dayego et al., 2025; PSA, 2024).

Why it keeps happening

Global cost pressures

International brand buyers demand steep cost cuts from tier-1 and tier-2 suppliers. Firms respond by treating labor as a variable operating expense (ILO, 2023).

Regulatory ambiguity

Article 106 permits legitimate job contracting. Its agency qualification criteria still let undercapitalized vendors act as labor suppliers without substantial capital equipment (Dayego et al., 2025).

Enforcement gaps

DOLE has about 800 to 900 compliance officers to monitor over 1.08 million registered establishments. The average establishment waits a long time between audits (DOLE-ILS, 2025).

What it costs workers: facts and positions

Judging the effects of short-term contracts means separating what the record verifies from what advocates argue.

Documented
  • Economic. Agency-hired contractual workers lack regular status. That excludes them from consistent SSS, PhilHealth and Pag-IBIG contributions and from the paperwork needed for bank credit or housing loans (Dayego et al., 2025; PSA, 2024).
  • Social. Fixed five-month rotations create predictable stretches of unpaid unemployment between agency placements (Dayego et al., 2025).
  • Political. International monitoring missions verified that union organizers trying to represent non-regular workers face administrative obstacles and legal intimidation (ILO, 2023; WRW, 2025).
  • Cultural. Long-standing contract hiring has normalized unstable work among entry-level job seekers (Dayego et al., 2025).
Position
  • Labor rights organizations argue that the barrier to credit and housing locks working-class households into generational cycles of poverty (WRW, 2025).
  • Observers argue that these restrictions reduce workers' freedom of association and weaken the collective bargaining protections guaranteed by international conventions (ILO, 2023).

Reading the claims critically

False claim

"A ban on short-term contractualization will cause SMEs to fail immediately."

Industry advocacy groups make this argument often. Comparative analyses show that labor overhead in SME service operations is a smaller share of fixed costs than real estate leases and utilities. Keeping regular workers can also offset wage costs through higher productivity and lower recruitment spending (DOLE-ILS, 2025; PSA, 2024). Presenting routine compliance as an existential threat is malinformation meant to stall reform.

Methodological limit

413,940 contractual workers became regular through DOLE compliance orders and commitment checks under Department Order 174 (DOLE-ILS, 2025). The number counts status changes that regional officers recorded during inspections. It has three blind spots:

  • It does not track whether workers stayed employed past 12 months.
  • It does not count workers later released in corporate restructurings.
  • It does not count workers quietly re-engaged under separate agencies after the audit ended (Dayego et al., 2025).

Open questions and lessons from abroad

An open question. What does full enforcement of direct hiring cost small and medium enterprises if no transition support is offered? Advocates call for ending labor-only subcontracting. The capacity of narrow-margin lower-tier suppliers still has to be assessed. Temporary tax credits, phased compliance periods and subsidized bridge financing are practical lines of inquiry to run alongside rights-based reform.

The wider picture. The Philippine experience matches a pattern across export-oriented economies in South Asia and Latin America, where global buyers push employment risk down the supply chain (European Parliament, 2024; ILO, 2023). Other regulators go further. The EU's Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive (CSDDD) legally obliges parent firms to monitor and address labor violations across their supply networks (European Parliament, 2024). Workers in Philippine supply chains still depend on local administrative labor tribunals, which leaves an accountability gap between multinational buyers and local subcontractors (ILO, 2023).

Who’s at the table

The debate sets constitutional worker protections against corporate flexibility. Three blocs argue it, and the courts have already settled part of it.

As global trade pushes domestic firms to cut overhead, the legal rules on employment arrangements have become the main battleground (ECOP, 2025; NAGKAISA Labor Coalition, 2025). To read that debate fairly, we sort the stakeholders, separate advocacy from established law, test common legal claims, and state how we judged our sources.

Three blocs, three agendas

Position

Ban trilateral contracting. Security of tenure is a right.

The NAGKAISA Labor Coalition brings together the Federation of Free Workers (FFW), the Trade Union Congress of the Philippines (TUCP), Bukluran ng Manggagawang Pilipino (BMP) and Kilusang Mayo Uno (KMU). The UP Law Center Workers' and Employees' Program (UP Law Center WEP) works alongside them. They advocate strict statutory prohibitions on trilateral contracting (NAGKAISA Labor Coalition, 2025; UP Law Center WEP, 2025).

They frame security of tenure as an inalienable human right under Article 23 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights and Article XIII, Section 3 of the 1987 Constitution. In their view, agency contracting is an artificial middleman scheme that depresses baseline wages, avoids fringe benefits and undermines union bargaining units (NAGKAISA Labor Coalition, 2025; UP Law Center WEP, 2025).

Position

Subcontracting is managerial prerogative. Bans cost jobs.

The Employers Confederation of the Philippines (ECOP), the Philippine Chamber of Commerce and Industry (PCCI) and the Information Technology and Business Process Association of the Philippines (IBPAP) argue for managerial discretion and operational agility (ECOP, 2025).

They present subcontracting as a normal way to handle swings in demand and stay globally competitive. ECOP's policy proposals contend that comprehensive bans on third-party job contracting would jeopardize foreign direct investment, reduce flexibility and threaten the viability of small and medium enterprises (ECOP, 2025).

Mandate

Balance statutory standards against economic development.

DOLE, the Congress of the Philippines and the Supreme Court act as mediators and enforcers. They are tasked with balancing statutory standards against economic development goals (Supreme Court of the Philippines, 2016, 2022).

What the court says and what each side says

Advocacy positions are arguments. Statute and case law are binding. The Supreme Court has ruled that when a worker does duties that are necessary and desirable to the principal's usual business or trade, direct regular employment attaches by operation of law (Magsalin v. NLRC, 1996; Quintanar v. Coca-Cola Bottlers Philippines, Inc., 2016; Supreme Court of the Philippines, 2022).

Advocacy
  • Labor: third-party contracting is a scheme to depress wages and weaken unions.
  • Employers: subcontracting is a managerial right, and bans threaten investment and SMEs.
Binding standard
  • Necessary and desirable work gives regular status by operation of law.
  • A contract between a principal and an agency cannot override the statutory test of employment status (Quintanar, 2016; Supreme Court of the Philippines, 2022).

The “agency is the only employer” myth

False claim

"The manpower agency is the only employer of contracted workers, so the principal business is immune from labor liability."

Industry representatives circulate this claim, because the worker signed with the agency (ECOP, 2025). Under Article 106 of the Labor Code it misstates the rules on labor-only contracting. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that where the agency lacks substantial capital equipment, or the principal controls how the work is done, labor-only contracting exists. The principal is then the direct employer by operation of law (Quintanar, 2016; Supreme Court of the Philippines, 2022).

Where we stand

We align with labor coalitions and human rights organizations. Security of tenure is a constitutional mandate, not a negotiable operational convenience (NAGKAISA Labor Coalition, 2025; UP Law Center WEP, 2025). "5-5-5" arrangements and non-compliant trilateral subcontracting work as structural workarounds around that right (Quintanar, 2016; Supreme Court of the Philippines, 2022). Treating labor as a variable commodity weakens worker security, depresses domestic consumption and restricts the constitutional rights to self-organization and collective bargaining (NAGKAISA Labor Coalition, 2025).

Headlines versus the fine print

Coverage of Endo tends to report the fight. It rarely explains the legal mechanism behind it.

Mainstream Philippine media frames "Endo" through political conflict instead of the legal mechanisms that sustain it (CMFR, 2018; Daily Tribune, 2025; UP College of Law, 2025). Coverage clusters around three perspectives.

Endo as corporate exploitation.

Shown through mass protests and reactions to vetoed legislation (CMFR, 2018; Daily Tribune, 2025).

Restrictions as a threat to jobs and competitiveness.

Presented through economic commentary on potential job losses, rising overhead and competitive vulnerability if outsourcing is restricted (ECOP, 2025).

Voluntary regularization as the headline.

Heavily mirrored from official press releases. They highlight regularization numbers and play down systemic loopholes (CMFR, 2018; DOLE-ILS, 2025).

When the numbers get spun

Reported discrepancy

Government channels cite optimistic job creation figures. Labor groups and academic think tanks, including the IBON Foundation and the UP Law Center WEP, challenge them as misleading malinformation. Their argument is that headline metrics count precarious, part-time and five-month agency roles as "employed", which hides an underlying job-precarity crisis (Daily Tribune, 2025; UP College of Law, 2025).

One claim, put to the test

False framingClassification: misinformation

"Banning labor contractualization will make all business outsourcing, including the BPO and IT sector, illegal under Philippine law."

This is strategic misinformation meant to discourage legislative reform. We verified it against existing labor jurisprudence and DOLE Department Order No. 174. Philippine law separates two things clearly (Magsalin v. NLRC, 1996; Supreme Court of the Philippines, 2022; UP College of Law, 2025):

ProhibitedLabor-only contracting (LOC)LegitimateJob contracting or BPO operations
Supplies workers who have no substantial capital or equipment. An independent entity with substantial capitalization above ₱5 million.
The workers perform core functions. The entity owns its operational equipment.
The principal directly controls the work. The entity exercises operational control.

Anti-Endo measures target the left-hand column. The claim that they would destroy the BPO industry misreads that distinction.

Head to head

What it does Bans third-party contracting for essential business functions and requires regular status from day one for core roles (UP Law Center WEP, 2025). Prohibits outsourcing roles central to a firm's primary business, such as retail floor clerks and assembly line workers, and requires immediate absorption onto the firm's payroll (UP Law Center WEP, 2025). Grants tax credits or enhanced deductions, for example an extra 50% deduction on direct labor expenses, to firms keeping a high share (for example 90%) of direct regular employees (ECOP, 2025).

Philippines: D.O. 174 "Main Business Test" and the Supreme Court four-fold control test. Abroad: Mexico's 2021 reform banned subcontracting for core activities and requires public REPSE registration for specialized non-core services. Indonesia (MOMR No. 7/2026) restricts outsourcing to a closed list of auxiliary services: security, catering, cleaning. Closest existing form is the Enhanced Deductions Regime of the CREATE Act (R.A. 11534) and CREATE MORE Act (R.A. 12066), administered through PEZA and BOI (ECOP, 2025).
Reach Nationwide across 17 administrative regions. Targets retail malls, fast food, electronics and agribusiness plantations (Dayego et al., 2025; DOLE-ILS, 2025). Applies to core roles across firms. Specialized non-core services stay allowed through a public registry. Registered Business Enterprises in export zones, electronics clusters and IT parks across CALABARZON, Metro Manila, Cebu and Mindanao (ECOP, 2025).
Structural challenges
  • Corporate appeals in the Court of Appeals and Supreme Court freeze regularization for years.
  • Inspectorate deficit: too few inspectors for over 1.08 million sites.
  • Capitalization workarounds: agencies inflate paid-up capital on paper to meet the ₱5 million requirement.
  • Firms rewrite bylaws and move core roles into separate subsidiaries.
  • State inspectorates are understaffed.
  • No targeted conditioning: write-offs for direct payroll do not require converting agency workers.
  • Voluntary: employers may ignore the credit if agency savings exceed it.
Best practices Unannounced joint audits with trade unions, strict use of the four-fold control test, joint and solidary liability for principals, and minimum capitalization. A central public registry (the REPSE model), high capital entry barriers, mandatory joint liability, and audits built around the four-fold control test (Dayego et al., 2025). BIR and DOLE cross-verification, credits tied to verified SSS, PhilHealth and Pag-IBIG remittances, and clawbacks when illegal contracting is found.
How success is measured Workers regularized (413,940 in 2016–2018), D.O. 174 contractor registrations cancelled, and back-pay recovered through NCMB conciliation. Direct-payroll transition rates, post-inspection compliance, fewer NLRC illegal dismissal filings, and 12-month retention after absorption. Agency workers converted, return on tax spending against formal wage growth, and inter-agency compliance certificates (ECOP, 2025; PSA, 2024).

The stress test

Four criteria drawn from the binding constraints of Philippine labor relations.

The yardsticks

Speed of implementation

How fast a mechanism can be deployed through existing administrative and financial channels, without years of legislative debate.

Economic sustainability

Whether it balances corporate viability and tax expenditure against fair living wages and social security coverage for workers.

Legal defensibility and permanence

How well it resists corporate appeals, court injunctions and political turnover.

Inclusivity and adaptability

How well it protects vulnerable worker groups across domestic supply chains while fitting different industry structures.

Follow the money

Smart Escrow Factory-Gate Financing: a legal ban that pays for itself in enforcement.

Legal bans and tax incentives each have real strengths. Neither closes the enforcement gap alone. Bans suffer from prolonged corporate litigation, and incentives are voluntary. Our proposal combines the legal clarity of Solution 2, outlawing core-function outsourcing, with an enforcement tool: Smart Escrow Factory-Gate Financing.

When a multinational lead firm or major domestic retailer places a purchase order, a fixed portion of the contract funds goes into a commercial bank escrow account governed by central bank rules (BSP Circular No. 982). The account then splits its payments automatically.

How we will know it worked

How the group will know Smart Escrow is working, written as a SMART goal: specific, measurable, achievable, relevant and time-bound.

The goal

Within 36 months of launch, at least 90% of core-role workers at suppliers enrolled in Smart Escrow are on direct regular payroll from day one, verified through SSS, PhilHealth and Pag-IBIG remittances. At least 85% of them are still employed 12 months after absorption. No escrow payment is released without that verification.

Proposed targets

Baselines come from the paper. The targets are the group's starting proposals, not findings. Review and adjust them before presenting.

Direct, regular jobs from day one, proven by remittances.

  • Who: core-role workers such as floor clerks, assembly line operators, warehouse handlers and farmworkers (PSA, 2024).
  • Where: suppliers that fill purchase orders for multinational lead firms and major domestic retailers.
  • What changes: five-month agency rolling for core roles is replaced by direct regular employment.
  • How: purchase-order funds sit in escrow and are released only against verified SSS, PhilHealth and Pag-IBIG remittances.

It runs on channels that already exist.

  • Existing rails: commercial bank escrow under central bank rules, SSS, PhilHealth and Pag-IBIG remittance records, DOLE's visitorial powers under Article 128, and CREATE MORE rebates under R.A. 12066 (DOLE-ILS, 2025; ECOP, 2025).
  • Light on inspectors: verification is digital, so DOLE steps in on exceptions instead of auditing every site. Today there are 800 to 900 officers for over 1.08 million establishments (DOLE-ILS, 2025).
  • Start small: a pilot cohort of suppliers comes before any wider rollout.
  • Open risk: transition costs for small suppliers on narrow margins. Phased compliance or bridge financing may be needed (Section 1).

It goes straight at the gap the paper found.

  • It puts the constitutional mandate of security of tenure into practice instead of leaving it to case-by-case litigation (NAGKAISA Labor Coalition, 2025).
  • It closes the weakness of both earlier options: bans stall in court, and tax incentives are voluntary.
  • It targets the 5-5-5 cycle directly, because funds are released only for direct, regular employment.
  • It holds buyers financially accountable, in the spirit of the EU's due diligence directive (European Parliament, 2024).

Permanent from day one

The argument in one paragraph.

The problem20–30%

of formal private-sector workers are agency-hired or non-regular, and 5-5-5 contracts end one month before regularization.

The gapStalled

Bans sit in court, tax breaks are voluntary, and 800 to 900 officers cover over 1.08 million sites.

The fixNo proof, no pay

Ban core-function outsourcing, and release payroll only against verified SSS, PhilHealth and Pag-IBIG remittances.

Systemic labor contractualization ("Endo") and anti-union precarity are driven by global supply-chain cost pressures that exploit gray areas in the Labor Code. An estimated 20% to 30% of the formal workforce is left without job security or social protection. Administrative inspections and legislative proposals have stalled against corporate litigation and understaffed inspectorates. The most effective response is a strict legal prohibition on outsourcing core operational functions, reinforced by Smart Escrow Factory-Gate Financing. By locking supply-chain purchase funds in commercial escrow and releasing payroll directly on digital proof of statutory benefit remittances, the mechanism holds multinational brands financially accountable, removes third-party agency exploitation, guarantees direct employment from day one and secures fundamental labor rights.
